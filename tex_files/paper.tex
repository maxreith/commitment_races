\section{Introduction}
As Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology continues to evolve, AI agents are becoming an increasingly prominent object of study. In the future, we may see capable AI agents operating under minimal oversight and engaging in strategic interactions. It has been suggested that such AI agents could possess the ability to make credible commitments \citep{hammond_multi-agent_2025}. There are concerns that if AI agents have such an ability, they may use this power to extort others. For example, one AI agent might commit to an aggressive action that then forces another AI agent to accommodate. But if both AI agents possess this ability, each may attempt to commit to the aggressive action first. This dynamic gives rise to what has been called a \textit{commitment race} \citep{kokotajlo_commitment_2019}, in which each agent races to commit before the other. If both commit simultaneously to incompatible strategies, the outcome could be harmful to both.

% In this paper, we develop a formal model to study this dynamic. 

% Before playing the base game, players have the opportunity to simultaneously commit to any normal-form strategy of this game.

In this paper, we study this dynamic game-theoretically. Two players play a game, referred to hereafter as the base game. Before playing the base game, players have the opportunity to simultaneously commit to any action of this game. Commitment is voluntary; players can choose not to commit and wait until the base game. We refer to the overall game as the commitment game. Our main result is that the ability to credibly commit can lead both players to be worse off than if neither had this ability. In particular, in the Game of Chicken, the unique symmetric equilibrium in the commitment game is Pareto inferior to the unique symmetric equilibrium in the base game. This arises because the non-aggressive strategy of Waiting in the commitment game becomes less attractive in equilibrium than the non-aggressive strategy of Moving in the base game, increasing the likelihood of simultaneous aggressive behavior. We illustrate this with an example.

\subsection*{Example}
Consider the well-known Game of Chicken depicted in Table~\ref{tab:gameofchicken}, where two players simultaneously decide whether to go Straight or Move (out of the way). Each player most prefers to go Straight themselves and for the other player to Move out of the way, but if both players go Straight they crash. Besides two pure-strategy Nash equilibria where one player plays Straight and the other plays Move, this game has one mixed strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE), in which both players choose Straight with probability $\baseProbStraight$ and Move with probability $\baseProbMove$, resulting in an equilibrium payoff of $\baseMixPayoff$. This is the unique symmetric equilibrium of the game, and we will refer to it as the base game equilibrium henceforth.

In the commitment game, there are multiple subgame‐perfect Nash equilibria (SPNE) in pure strategies: in one, one player commits straight and the other waits and then moves out of the way; in another, one player commits straight and the other commits to moving out of the way. Importantly, no equilibrium can involve both players waiting before choosing a pure strategy equilibrium, since in that case one player would end up in her less‐preferred equilibrium and could profitably deviate by committing straight. 
In addition to these pure strategy SPNE, there exists a unique mixed strategy SPNE.\footnote{For clarity, only equilibria involving both-sided probabilistic commitment are referred to as mixed equilibria, even though pure strategy equilibria are technically also mixed strategy equilibria.} Here, each player commits to the aggressive strategy (Straight) with probability $\ToneProbCommitStraight$ and waits with probability $\ToneWait$. If both players decide to wait, they play the base game equilibrium. This is the unique symmetric SPNE of the commitment game. Notably, this equilibrium has an expected payoff of $\TonePayoff$ for each player,  while the base game equilibrium has an expected payoff of $\baseMixPayoff$ for each player.

Why is that? As it turns out, the ability to commit one period before transforms any Game of Chicken into \textit{another} Game of Chicken with strictly lower payoffs for both players, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:introduction-intuition}. In the commitment game, committing to $M$ (moving out of the way) is weakly dominated by $W$ (waiting) and there is no symmetric equilibrium where committing to $M$ is played with positive probability. Hence, we can remove it. $(S, W)$ gives payoffs $(\capB, \capC)$ since player 2 will best respond with $M$ in the next period, $(W, S)$ gives the reverse, $(W, W)$ gives payoffs $(\baseMixPayoff,\baseMixPayoff)$, the base game equilibrium payoffs, and $(S, S)$ gives payoffs $(-10, -10)$, the payoff of crashing. The commitment game is hence another Game of Chicken, except with $(\baseMixPayoff,\baseMixPayoff)$ rather than $(\capD,\capD)$ in the bottom right cell. Thus, playing the aggressive strategy $S$ becomes relatively more attractive in the commitment game than in the base game, leading to more aggressive behavior, a higher probability of crashing, and lower payoffs for both players in equilibrium.

In fact, the logic of the proof is straightforward and instructive. In the base game, in any MSNE each player must be indifferent between playing $S$ and $M$. $S$ results in a mixture between $(S, M)$ (win) and $(S, S)$ (crash)—the best and worst outcomes. $M$ results in a mixture between $(M, M)$ (safe) and $(M, S)$ (lose) —the middle two outcomes. Hence, $\text{win} \succ \text{safe} \succ \text{base game} \succ \text{lose} \succ \text{crash}$.

In the commitment game, in any mixed strategy SPNE each player must be indifferent between playing $S$ and $W$, since $M$ is weakly dominated by $W$. Playing $S$ results in a mixture between $(S, W \to M)$ (win) and $(S, S)$ (crash)—the best and worst outcomes. Playing $W$ results in a mixture between $(W, W)$ (playing the base game) and $(W \to M, S)$ (lose). Hence, $\text{win} \succ \text{safe} \succ \text{base game} \succ \text{commitment game} \succ \text{lose} \succ \text{crash}$, and the commitment game equilibrium has a strictly \emph{lower} expected payoff than the base game equilibrium.

Up until now, we have considered a single commitment period before the base game during which players can simultaneously commit. However, if players can commit to a strategy for a game they will play in the future, it seems natural that they might be able to do so at multiple points prior to play. This is particularly relevant if one thinks of the players as AI agents. Fast computational speed could imply that agents have many, effectively infinite, opportunities to commit beforehand.
Let $T$ represent the number of discrete commitment periods. In each commitment period, players simultaneously decide whether to commit to a strategy or wait. If both players commit, their chosen strategies are executed immediately. If only one player commits, the other observes this commitment and best responds. If both players wait, the game advances to the next commitment period, or to the base game if no commitment periods remain. 

% Having examined the case of a single period ($T=1$), we now consider the case of multiple periods ($T>1$).

What happens as we increase the number of commitment periods $T$ in the Game of Chicken? In the unique symmetric SPNE of the commitment game, the expected payoff for both players strictly decreases with $T$ and, as $T$ approaches infinity, converges to each player's minmax payoff of the base game---the payoff of the worst possible equilibrium outcome. Table~\ref{tab:multiple-commitment-periods-chicken} shows the expected payoffs and the probability of crashing as $T$ increases.

The mechanism behind this result is identical to the single-period case. Each additional commitment period transforms the game into a new Game of Chicken with lower payoffs. Waiting becomes relatively less attractive, making early commitments to Straight more likely, which increases the probability of crashing and lowers the expected payoffs for both players. In fact, the probability of commitment in \textit{every} period increases with $T$, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:commitment-probabilities}.

The term commitment race was originally introduced by \citet{kokotajlo_commitment_2019} in the context of AI agents potentially extorting one another. While his treatment is highly informal, our contribution here is to formalize this idea within a game-theoretic framework.

We refer to this dynamic as a \textit{commitment race}. A commitment race is when two agents each attempt to be the first to lock in the action associated with their preferred equilibrium, so that the best response of the other is to concede. 
In this paper, we study commitment races in undominated symmetric equilibria in all symmetric $2\times2$ games. The three main takeaways are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In the Game of Chicken, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and decreases the welfare of both players.
    \item In the Battle of the Sexes, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and increases the welfare of both players.
    \item In all other symmetric $2\times2$ games, commitment races do not occur.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Literature}
In the game theoretic literature, commitment is often associated with welfare-enhancing outcomes, at least for the player making the commitment. Commitment features prominently across a wide range of models, such as principals committing to mechanisms, players delegating decision-making to agents, and first movers in Stackelberg games earning a higher profit \citep[see the discussion in][]{kalai_commitment_2010}. This paper, however, turns to the question of whether the ability to make commitments can \textit{harm} players.

Existing literature has largely focused on one-sided commitment, leaving two-sided commitment relatively understudied. \citet{harrenstein_commitment_2007} analyze two- and three-player games in which all players have the ability to commit. However, the authors assume a predetermined order of commitment and do not allow for simultaneous commitment.

\citet{kalai_commitment_2010} present a model of simultaneous conditional commitment, expanding on earlier work by \citet{tennenholtz_program_2004}. In their framework, players select commitment devices $d$ before the base game. A commitment device specifies a base game strategy as a function of the other's chosen commitment device, $d_i(d_{-i}) = s_i$. The authors' main result is a commitment folk theorem, stating that the Nash equilibria in such commitment games span all individually-rational correlated strategy payoffs of the base game. Naturally, this result relies on the availability of a complete space of conditional commitment devices. In contrast, our model considers the case when players can commit only to some action in the base game. 

% In contrast, our model limits players to committing exclusively to normal form strategies. This still allows for some conditionality in sequential base games. For example in an entrant-incumbent game, the incumbent's normal form strategies allow commitment that conditions on the entrant's initial decision whether to enter the market or not.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section \ref{sec:commitment-in-symmetric-2x2-games} investigates commitment races in all symmetric $2\times2$ games, first with a single period of commitment and then with multiple periods. Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{One Commitment Period}\label{sec:one-commitment-period}

We first consider the case of one commitment period in all symmetric $2\times2$ games.

\begin{proposition}\label{theo:GamePayoffs}
Consider a symmetric $2\times2$ game and its commitment variant.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In the Game of Chicken, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and decreases the welfare of both players.
    \item In the Battle of the Sexes, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and increases the welfare of both players.
    \item In all other symmetric $2\times2$ games, commitment races do not occur. In particular, commitment introduces no new undominated symmetric equilibria.
\end{enumerate}

In the Game of Chicken, commitment makes players worse off; in contrast, it improves outcomes in the Battle of the Sexes. To our knowledge, this finding is not documented in the existing literature.

Proposition~\ref{theo:GamePayoffs} shows that commitment races occur in some games and don't occur in others, and that when they do occur they can make the players better off or worse off. \subsection{Multiple Commitment Periods}\label{sec:multiple-commitment-periods}
% In this section, we extend the model to allow for multiple discrete commitment periods, denoted by $T$.
% more general
We now consider the case of $T$ commitment periods in all symmetric $2\times2$ games. If player $1$ commits in the first commitment period and player $2$ waits, then player $2$ gets to observe $1$'s commitment in the second period, and best responds to it.
If instead both players do not commit in the first period, they observe each other's inaction and then in the second period simultaneously decide again whether to commit. If they do not commit throughout all the $T$ commitment periods, they arrive at the base game and must choose between $A_1$ or $A_2$.

Let $A_{1t}$ denote the strategy in which a player commits to $A_1$ in period $t$. Let $W$ denote the strategy in which a player waits in every period and plays the base game equilibrium when reached. Note that in either case, if the other player commits first, the player best responds to that commitment.

% Let $A_{1t}$ denote the strategy whereby a player commits to $A_1$ in period $t$, unless the other player has already committed in an earlier period, in which case, the player best responds instead of committing. Let $W$ denote the Waiting strategy, which means not committing in any commitment period, best-responding to any commitment made by the other player, and if the base game is reached playing the MSNE strategy of the base game.
Let $p_{t}$ denote the (ex ante) probability of a player playing $A_{1t}$ and $q_t$ the probability of a player playing $A_{2t}$. Let $\sigma(W)=1-\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}(p_\tau+q_\tau)$ denote the probability of waiting.

\begin{proposition}\label{theo:convergence}
    Consider a symmetric $2\times2$ game and its commitment variant with $T$ periods.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item In the Game of Chicken, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and decreases the welfare of both players towards $\cparam$, the payoff each player receives from losing, as $T \to \infty$.
        \item In the Battle of the Sexes, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and increases the welfare of both players towards $\min\{\bparam,\cparam\}$, the payoff each player receives from coordinating on their second choice destination, as $T \to \infty$.
        \item In all other symmetric $2\times2$ games, commitment races do not occur. In particular, commitment introduces no new undominated symmetric equilibria.
    \end{enumerate}

\end{proposition}

We know from Proposition \ref{theo:GamePayoffs} that applying a round of commitment to the Game of Chicken decreases the equilibrium payoffs for both players. Moreover, in the discussion of the Game of Chicken in the introduction, we saw that the equilibrium payoffs of the commitment game are bounded above by the equilibrium payoffs of the base game and bounded below by the payoff of losing. Proposition 2 shows that repeated addition of commitment rounds continues to decrease the equilibrium payoffs and that they converge to the payoff of losing as $T \to \infty$.

Analogously, we know from Proposition \ref{theo:GamePayoffs} that applying a round of commitment to the Battle of the Sexes increases the equilibrium payoffs for both players. Moreover, in the discussion following we saw that the equilibrium payoffs of the commitment game are bounded above by the payoff of coordinating on the second choice and bounded below by the equilibrium payoffs of the base game. Proposition 2 shows that repeated addition of commitment rounds continues to increase the equilibrium payoffs and that they converge to the payoff of coordinating on the second choice as $T \to \infty$.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A commitment race is when two agents each attempt to be the first to lock in the action associated with their preferred equilibrium, so that the best response of the other is to concede. This paper studies commitment races in undominated symmetric equilibria in all symmetric $2\times2$ games, with $T$ commitment periods. We find that in the Game of Chicken, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and decreases the welfare of both players; in the Battle of the Sexes, a commitment race occurs in the unique symmetric equilibrium and increases the welfare of both players; and in all other symmetric $2\times2$ games, commitment races do not occur. Moreover, when commitment races are present, early commitment becomes more likely as $T$ grows, and its effect on welfare becomes increasingly amplified.

% Original
% This paper develops a model of simultaneous commitment, focusing on the possibility that commitment can lead to worse outcomes. In the model, players can voluntarily commit to normal-form strategies before playing a base game. We show that in some symmetric $2 \times 2$ games, the option to commit strictly reduces equilibrium payoffs. We characterize symmetric $2 \times 2$ games for which commitment reduces or improves equilibrium payoffs and extend the model to multiple commitment periods. As $T \to \infty$, early incompatible commitments can become more likely, and payoffs can decline further.

\paragraph{Some limitations remain.}

% First, the finding that commitment can worsen outcomes relies on mixed-strategy Nash equilibria as the equilibrium concept. We focus on mixed-strategy equilibria because they capture the possibility of miscoordination and allow for quantifying its likelihood. Moreover, in symmetric games, mixed equilibria may be the only symmetric equilibria available. However, this justification does not extend to asymmetric games.

Firstly, we study only symmetric games, and only the undominated symmetric equilibria of those games. Additional dynamics may arise in asymmetric games or with asymmetric equilibria. Secondly, we study only $2\times2$ games. Analyzing larger games may reveal additional dynamics that are not present here. Finally, while motivated by concerns over AI agents capable of making commitments, this paper does not explore technical implementation. An interesting direction for further research is to explore in what ways commitment in AI agents can be designed to avoid the inefficient outcomes identified in this paper. Conditional commitments \citep[]{kalai_commitment_2010} or simulation-based program equilibria \citep[]{cooper_characterising_2024} seem like promising directions here.
